I
Chapter one

Money

2014Bengaluru. A founder, a bank, and a six-week wait.

For most of history, money has moved slower than the people who needed it.

SuratWeeks

A merchant is awaiting receipt of the letter of credit issued by a bank in London.

Nashik3 days

A farmer waiting for a cheque to clear.

Bengaluru6 weeks

A founder waiting on a bank relationship manager's email to become a working integration.

That founder's name was Rohit Taneja. The company was Mypoolin. The problem wasn't the idea. The problem was that every fintech idea in India, at the time, had the same first requirement: before you could build a product, you had to become a systems integrator. You had to negotiate with banks one at a time. Reconcile ledgers by hand. Rebuild KYC from scratch. Stitch together a dozen disconnected vendors just to move money from one account to another — and pray the stitching held.

Mypoolin got acquired by PayU. The lesson didn't leave with it.

We accepted that building a fintech company meant spending your first eighteen months as a plumber, not a product builder. We accepted that "bank-grade" meant "bureaucracy-grade." We accepted that innovation in financial services would always be rationed out by whichever institution was willing to answer your call.

We reject that reality. Money should move as freely as information does. It doesn't yet. We intend to be the reason it does.

II
Chapter two

The Cost of Friction

1 hourspent integrating with a bank is an hour not spent serving a customer.

Every hour a person spends integrating with a bank is an hour they don't spend serving a customer.

Many fintech companies that fail aren't failing at their product. They're failing at plumbing they never should have had to build. The real tragedy of financial infrastructure in emerging markets isn't that it's absent. It's that it exists, scattered across hundreds of banks, payment rails, KYC providers and compliance regimes, each speaking a different language, each demanding its own integration, its own contract, its own six-month waiting period.

Friction like this is invisible until you measure what it costs. It costs the market its most promising builders, who quietly give up before they ship. It costs consumers years of delay on products that already work in other markets. It costs India the chance to lead the next wave of financial inclusion, when it has already proven it can lead the last one.

UPI

UPI didn't win because it was the only idea. It won because, for the first time, the rails were unified and the friction disappeared. We watched that happen and asked a simple question: what if every layer of finance — not just payments — worked that way?

The size of the fintech industry a market can support is set by how much friction stands between an idea and a working product.

Lower the friction, and you make companies possible that weren't possible before.

An underwriting company

A credit model assembled from bureau pulls and spreadsheets.

no linkvendor 04

A payments company

A payment gateway from one more vendor, one more contract.

no linkday one of growth

A collections company

Starts the moment your growth curve does — usually before you're ready for it.

And you ship a lending product only after all six survive contact with each other. This is the world most fintech executives still build in.

It is not a technology problem. Every one of these pieces has existed, individually, for years. It is an infrastructure problem — the absence of a single fabric that lets all of it work as one system instead of six.
